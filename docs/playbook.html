This is the single most useful PM habit. The status board is not a progress report, it is a decision tool. The moment "measurement is broken" is written down next to "scanner works," the next move stops being a matter of taste.

The core move

Build outcomes, not features

The most common mistake is describing work as the thing you will build. A product manager describes it as the change for the person using it. Same work, completely different decisions fall out.

"Add a Gmail integration" is a feature. "I never lose track of an application again" is an outcome. The second one tells you when you are done, tells you what to cut, and tells you that a plain text file listing every reply would count as a win if it produced that result.

For ALYX right now the outcome is not "more applications." It is more live conversations with employers, and knowing which ones are live. Every feature below either serves that or gets cut.

The reusable part

Five questions, in this order

Run these on any feature anyone proposes, including yourself at 1am. The order matters, because a yes to a later question cannot rescue a no to an earlier one.

  1. What breaks if I do nothing?

    If the honest answer is "nothing," it is not the next thing. Applications silently vanishing is a real break. A prettier dashboard is not.

  2. Who is this for, and how do I know they want it?

    Right now the user is you, which makes this easy and dangerous. Easy because you can ask. Dangerous because "I would think this is cool" is not evidence.

  3. What has to be true for this to work?

    The riskiest assumption, not the hardest code. Auto-applying assumes unreviewed answers are accurate enough to send under your name. That assumption is the whole feature, and it is the part most likely to be wrong.

  4. What does this unblock?

    Some work is worth doing mostly because it makes three other things possible. Reading your inbox is that. Assisted apply needs to know what you already sent.

  5. What is the smallest version that proves it?

    Not the smallest version you would ship. The smallest one that tells you whether the idea holds. Usually a fraction of what you first imagined.

Why auto-submit scores low on impact, not just high on risk. Your problem was never volume. You applied 50 times and had six real conversations, and the system recorded almost none of them. Sending 600 applications into a pipeline you cannot measure produces more noise and the same blindness.

The plan

Now, Next, Later

Three buckets, no dates. Dates on a solo project you build around shifts and interviews are fiction, and missing them just makes you feel behind. Order is the real commitment.

Now

one thing at a time, finished before the next starts
  • Read the inbox, propose the updates. Match confirmations and replies to applications, then show you a batch to approve with the source email cited on every proposed change. Never silent auto-writes, because a wrong status you did not see is worse than no status.
  • Backfill what is missing. The 13 untracked August applications and six interview processes, recovered and entered.

Next

only after Now is actually done
  • Answer library. Every screening question you have ever answered, stored once, reused. This is the piece of the auto-apply idea genuinely worth having.
  • Follow-up nudges. You have 25 applications quiet for 30 days or more. The system knows. It should tell you.
  • Fill the form, stop at submit. Everything up to the button, then you review and send.

Later

real, just not now
  • Desktop app on this data layer. The UI exists but reads an old format.
  • Per-role resume tailoring.
  • Deploy the landing page, once there is a repo behind its only button.

Not doing

written down so it stops coming back
  • Auto-submitting applications. Unreviewed answers to questions about work authorization, salary and experience go out under your name, and every major applicant tracking system prohibits it.
  • Making it a product for other people. Not until it has worked for one person for a full search.

The habit

A twenty minute loop, once a week

This is the entire job, repeated. It works whether you have four hours that week or none.

  1. Update the status board

    What actually works today. Be unkind about it. Half-finished counts as not working.

  2. Ask what broke or changed

    New information beats old plans. The Gmail discovery reordered this entire roadmap in one afternoon.

  3. Pick exactly one thing for Now

    One. Two things in Now means neither gets finished, and unfinished work teaches you nothing.

  4. Define what done looks like

    Written down, before you start. "It reads Gmail and I approved 20 updates" is done. "Gmail integration" is not.

  5. Move everything else to Next or Later

    Nothing gets deleted, which is what makes it possible to let go of it this week.

Worth knowing anyway

The words, decoded

These come up in every product conversation, and in interviews for product roles.

Scope
Everything a piece of work includes. "Cutting scope" means shipping less of it, not doing it worse. The distinction is the whole skill.
MVP, minimum viable product
The smallest version that proves whether the idea works. Frequently misused to mean "the cheap version," which is how products end up bad rather than small.
Riskiest assumption
The belief that, if wrong, makes the whole feature pointless. Test it first, cheaply, before building around it.
Leading and lagging indicators
Lagging is the result you want and cannot control, like getting hired. Leading is what you can move this week that tends to produce it, like conversations started. Track both, work the leading one.
Definition of done
Written before the work starts. Without it, work expands until you are exhausted rather than finished.
Technical debt
A shortcut taken deliberately that will cost more later. Fine when named and chosen. Expensive when it just happens.
