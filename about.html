Why this exists

I ran recruiting for operations roles across more than twenty US markets, built the batch interview process that became the company standard, and spent years telling candidates how to run a job search.

Then I ran one myself and found my own tracker had quietly lost 46 of 53 applications. It stored a single status per row, so every interview that ended in a rejection stopped existing. I had to rebuild the whole thing out of old email.

That is a recruiting problem, not a software one. The tools assume the only thing worth recording is the outcome. What I wanted to know was the shape of the search: which stage things died at, which version of my resume got replies, what was quietly going stale while I was not looking.

So I built it. ALYX is the result, and the design decisions in it come from the recruiting side rather than the engineering side. It proposes instead of deciding, because I have seen what a confidently wrong ATS record does to a candidate. It stops before submitting, because I have read the screening answers people send by accident.

How it was built

Every line of ALYX was written with Claude. I am not a software engineer and I have no formal background in it. I could not have written the ATS scanners or the Chrome extension by hand, and I am not going to pretend otherwise.

What I brought was the other half. Knowing that a tracker storing one status per row would lose the interviews. Knowing that a tool filling in screening answers must stop before it submits. Knowing that a matcher confidently proposing the wrong company is worse than one that asks. Those are judgments from five years of watching hiring processes go wrong, and they are the decisions that make the difference between this and a demo.

It is a fair question whether that counts as building something. I think it does, and I think the work was mostly in knowing what to insist on and what to refuse.
